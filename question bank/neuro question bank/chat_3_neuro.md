# NPTE Neuro Review – Neuromuscular & Clinical Conditions

**Q1.** A 50-year-old office worker presents with chronic neck pain and bilateral hand tingling that worsens after prolonged typing. Symptoms improve with rest and postural correction. Examination reveals tight scalenes and upper trapezius muscles, tenderness in the cervical region, and positive Tinel's sign at both wrists. Which diagnosis BEST explains compression occurring at both the cervical region and the wrist along the same peripheral nerve pathway?
A. Carpal tunnel syndrome – Isolated distal compression of the median nerve at the wrist
B. Cervical radiculopathy – Nerve root compression at the cervical spine only
C. Double crush syndrome – Proximal and distal compression impairing axoplasmic flow along the same nerve
D. Thoracic outlet syndrome – Compression of the brachial plexus between the scalene muscles only

**Correct Answer:**
- **C.** Double crush syndrome occurs when proximal compression impairs axoplasmic transport, making the distal portion of the nerve more vulnerable to additional compression. The patient demonstrates nerve compression at two distinct anatomical sites along the same neural pathway — cervical region tightness suggesting proximal irritation and positive bilateral Tinel's sign at the wrist indicating distal median nerve compression.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Carpal tunnel syndrome alone does not explain proximal cervical findings. While bilateral Tinel's sign supports distal median nerve compression, isolated CTS would not account for neck pain, scalene tightness, or cervical tenderness.
- **B.** Cervical radiculopathy alone does not explain positive Tinel's sign at the wrist. Radiculopathy originates at the nerve root level and would not produce a localized distal compression sign at the carpal tunnel.
- **D.** Thoracic outlet syndrome involves proximal brachial plexus compression but does not explain distal median nerve irritation at the wrist. The presence of bilateral positive Tinel's sign suggests a second compression site, which TOS alone cannot account for.

**Q2.** A 70-year-old patient is undergoing rehabilitation following a stroke. During therapy, the therapist observes that the patient has just begun to show minimal voluntary movement responses in the affected limbs, along with the development of spasticity. Based on Brunnstrom's stages of motor recovery, which stage is the patient MOST likely experiencing?
A. Stage 1
B. Stage 2
C. Stage 3
D. Stage 4

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** Stage 2 is characterized by the appearance of basic limb synergies or minimal voluntary movement responses, along with the onset of spasticity, which aligns with the patient's current condition.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Stage 1 involves flaccidity immediately following the stroke, with no movement of the limbs elicited.
- **C.** Stage 3 involves gaining voluntary control of movement synergies, but the patient has not yet reached this level.
- **D.** Stage 4 is characterized by the mastery of more complex movement combinations and a decline in spasticity, which is beyond the current observation.

**Q3.** A patient presents with radiating arm pain and glove-like numbness in bilateral hands. Hypomobility of the upper thoracic spine was noted during manual assessment. What is the most likely diagnosis?
A. Cervical radiculopathy
B. T4 syndrome
C. Thoracic disc herniation
D. Brachial plexopathy

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** T4 syndrome involves upper extremity pain, paresthesia, and autonomic symptoms originating from the upper thoracic spine. The symptoms may be reproduced by passive movement of the T4 vertebra.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Cervical radiculopathy typically follows a dermatomal pattern and is associated with positive Spurling's test, which is not mentioned here.
- **C.** Thoracic disc herniation is rare and often presents with localized back pain rather than glove-like numbness.
- **D.** Brachial plexopathy would likely have a traumatic or compressive etiology, with weakness more prominent than sensory disturbances.

**Q4.** A patient demonstrates an exaggerated response to gentle palpation, superficial tenderness, and pain elicited during simulated spine rotation tests. According to Waddell's criteria, these findings suggest:
A. Radicular nerve compression
B. Discogenic pain syndrome
C. Psychological or nonorganic pain
D. Lumbar spinal stenosis

**Correct Answer:**
- **C.** According to Waddell's tests, positive responses in three or more specific areas (e.g., superficial tenderness, exaggerated pain responses, inconsistent test results) suggest psychological or nonorganic causes for low back pain.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Radicular compression typically presents with consistent and specific nerve root findings rather than exaggerated responses.
- **B.** Discogenic pain generally produces consistent, anatomically logical responses, not exaggerated or superficial tenderness.
- **D.** Stenosis generally has reproducible symptoms related to position and activity, not exaggerated nonanatomic responses.

**Q5.** A 62-year-old patient with left hemiplegia demonstrates knee hyperextension while ambulating with a lightweight posterior leaf-spring brace that fails to control this motion. Which intervention is most appropriate to decrease the patient's knee hyperextension?
A. Continue with the current brace and focus on quadriceps strengthening.
B. Switch to a solid ankle brace to provide distal stability.
C. Use a knee immobilizer during ambulation.
D. Implement a carbon fibre AFO for increased dorsiflexion.

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** A solid ankle brace enhances ankle stability, which helps control knee hyperextension during ambulation by blocking plantarflexion and preventing the tibia from falling backward.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Quadriceps strengthening alone does not provide immediate control of hyperextension and is insufficient without appropriate bracing.
- **C.** A knee immobilizer is not practical; it restricts knee movement and limits functional mobility.
- **D.** A carbon fibre AFO improves foot position but does not directly address knee hyperextension.

**Q6.** A 45-year-old male patient presents to the physical therapy clinic with complaints of severe lower back pain that started a week ago after lifting a heavy object. He reports progressive weakness in one leg and describes a "pins and needles" sensation in the saddle region. Additionally, the patient has experienced difficulty controlling his bladder over the past two days. Upon further questioning, he mentions some difficulty with bowel movements as well. What is the most appropriate action for the physical therapist to take next?
A. Continue with conservative treatment, including manual therapy and core strengthening exercises, while monitoring the patient's symptoms for further changes.
B. Recommend over-the-counter anti-inflammatory medication and a follow-up in one week to reassess symptom progression.
C. Immediately refer the patient to an emergency department for medical evaluation due to the possibility of cauda equina syndrome.
D. Instruct the patient to perform lower back stretches at home and schedule a follow-up appointment in two weeks.

**Correct Answer:**
- **C.** Cauda equina syndrome is a medical emergency characterized by lower back pain, saddle anesthesia, and bowel or bladder dysfunction due to compression of the cauda equina nerves. Immediate referral is crucial to prevent permanent neurological damage.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Conservative treatment such as manual therapy and exercises is inappropriate for a patient with red flag symptoms indicative of CES. Delaying proper medical evaluation could lead to permanent neurological damage.
- **B.** Recommending medication and waiting to reassess could result in a dangerous delay in treatment. CES requires immediate medical intervention, typically surgery, to relieve pressure on the cauda equina nerves.
- **D.** Suggesting stretches and a delayed follow-up is inappropriate when the patient presents with symptoms indicative of a neurological emergency. Exercises could exacerbate the condition and waiting two weeks could result in permanent impairment.

**Q7.** A patient is diagnosed with carpal tunnel syndrome. Which sensory distribution would NOT typically be affected?
A. Distal thumb and index finger
B. Proximal palmar skin area
C. Middle and lateral half of ring finger
D. Palmar surface of thumb

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** Sensory changes do not affect the proximal palmar skin due to the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve passing superficial to the flexor retinaculum, hence not compressed in carpal tunnel syndrome.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** The distal thumb and index finger are typically affected in median nerve compression at the carpal tunnel.
- **C.** The middle and lateral half of the ring finger commonly experiences sensory disturbances in median nerve compression.
- **D.** The palmar surface of the thumb typically experiences sensory loss with carpal tunnel syndrome.

**Q8.** Which outcome measure is specifically designed to evaluate both static and dynamic balance in patients, making it a valuable tool for assessing fall risk?
A. Functional Gait Assessment (FGA)
B. Activities-specific Balance Confidence (ABC) Scale
C. Berg Balance Scale (BBS)
D. 10-Meter Walk Test (10mWT)

**Correct Answer:**
- **C.** The BBS is specifically designed to assess both static and dynamic balance through various tasks, such as standing, reaching, and turning. It is widely used to evaluate fall risk in older adults and individuals with balance impairments.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** The FGA assesses gait and balance during walking tasks but is not solely focused on static and dynamic balance as a standalone assessment. It incorporates dynamic components of gait.
- **B.** The ABC Scale measures an individual's confidence in performing various activities without falling.
- **D.** The 10mWT primarily measures walking speed over a short distance.

**Q9.** During a physical therapy session, a 16-year-old patient is learning a new task that requires coordinated movement of various components. The therapist employs a technique that focuses on improving coordination by initiating movement from distal to proximal components. The therapist restricts the activation of proximal components until the distal components are engaged. What technique is being described in this scenario?
A. Rhythmic Stabilization
B. Normal Timing (NT)
C. Slow Reversal
D. Agonistic Reversal

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** Normal Timing (NT) is a method used to enhance the coordination of various components in a task. It involves initiating movement from distal to proximal components, with proximal components restricted until the distal ones are activated. This promotes a coordinated and sequential movement pattern.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Rhythmic Stabilization involves alternating isometric contractions and is not specifically focused on the distal-to-proximal sequencing described in the scenario.
- **C.** Slow Reversal is a technique where the patient performs the concentric and eccentric phases of a movement against resistance, but it does not emphasize the distal-to-proximal sequence.
- **D.** Agonistic Reversal involves alternating between concentric and eccentric contractions of the agonist muscle group, and it does not follow the described pattern of initiating movement from distal to proximal.

**Q10.** A 45-year-old male patient presents to the physical therapy clinic with complaints of severe weakness of specific muscles, increasing fatigue, and mild to moderate functional limitations, while still being ambulatory. The symptoms include atrophy, cramping, muscle fasciculations, and dysarthria. What is the likely diagnosis, and at which stage is she most likely to be categorized in this progressive disease, often leading to death, typically in 2-5 years, with highly variable symptoms?
A. ALS, Stage II
B. Guillain-Barré Syndrome, Stage III
C. Multiple Sclerosis, Stage IV
D. Myasthenia Gravis, Stage V

**Correct Answer:**
- **A.** The described symptoms, including severe weakness, atrophy, cramping, muscle fasciculations, and dysarthria, are consistent with Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS). Given the progressive nature of the disease, the patient is likely in Stage II, characterized by moderate weakness in groups of muscles with some wasting (atrophy), and maintaining modified independence with assistive devices.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **B.** Guillain-Barré Syndrome is an acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy characterized by ascending weakness. The symptoms of ALS, including atrophy, cramping, and dysarthria, are more indicative of ALS rather than GBS. Furthermore, GBS tends to have a rapid onset and is not a progressive, long-term disease like ALS.
- **C.** Multiple Sclerosis is a chronic autoimmune disease affecting the central nervous system. The symptoms described, especially those related to severe weakness and progressive functional limitations, are not typical of MS. Additionally, MS does not have a staging system similar to ALS.
- **D.** Myasthenia Gravis is characterized by muscle weakness and fatigue, primarily affecting voluntary muscles. The provided symptoms and staging do not align with the typical progression of Myasthenia Gravis.

**Q11.** A patient has significant difficulty processing visual motion, depth, and color perception. Which Brodmann area is most likely damaged?
A. Area 17
B. Area 18
C. Area 19
D. Area 22

**Correct Answer:**
- **C.** Area 19 (tertiary visual cortex) is located in the medial and lateral occipital gyri and processes complex visual information, such as motion, depth, and color. A lesion in Area 19 could result in impairments like motion blindness (akinetopsia), where the patient struggles to perceive moving objects, or difficulty in recognizing color contrasts and spatial relationships.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Area 17 is the primary visual cortex and processes basic visual stimuli, such as light and dark.
- **B.** Area 18 is the secondary visual cortex and handles intermediate visual processing but not complex motion or depth.
- **D.** Area 22 is associated with higher-order auditory processing, not vision.

**Q12.** A patient with a history of epilepsy is undergoing physical therapy. The therapist notes that the patient is taking an antiepileptic medication. Considering the potential side effects of antiepileptic agents, what should be a particular focus for the physical therapist in managing this patient's care?
A. Cardiovascular monitoring due to risk of hypertension.
B. Regular assessment of skin condition and immediate reporting of any abnormalities.
C. Encouraging high-intensity exercises to enhance overall fitness.
D. Maintaining a well-lit environment to reduce seizure triggers.

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** Antiepileptic agents, depending on the specific drug, may have side effects that include skin issues. Physical therapists need to be vigilant in assessing the patient's skin regularly and promptly reporting any abnormalities to the healthcare team. Skin-related side effects can vary and may include rashes or other dermatological manifestations.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Antiepileptic medications are not typically associated with a direct risk of hypertension, and monitoring cardiovascular parameters is not a primary focus related to these medications.
- **C.** While exercise is generally beneficial, the focus should be on the potential side effects of the antiepileptic medication. High-intensity exercises might not be suitable for everyone, and the patient's individual response to exercise should be considered.
- **D.** Environmental factors like light can trigger seizures in some individuals with epilepsy. However, maintaining a well-lit environment is not a specific management strategy related to the potential side effects of antiepileptic agents.

**Q13.** A 45-year-old patient with traumatic brain injury is being assessed for cognitive functioning using the Rancho Los Amigos Levels of Cognitive Functioning. The patient is currently demonstrating purposeful and appropriate behavior with stand-by assistance. Which Rancho Los Amigos level is most likely represented by this cognitive functioning?
A. Level I
B. Level III
C. Level VI
D. Level VIII

**Correct Answer:**
- **D.** Level VIII corresponds with the patient's current level of cognitive functioning, exhibiting purposeful and appropriate behavior with a need for stand-by assistance. At this level, individuals can independently complete familiar tasks in non-distracting environments, are consistently oriented to person, place, and time, and recognize and minimally require assistance for their impairments.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Level I denotes a state of complete absence of observable behavioral change, not matching the patient's current functioning.
- **B.** Level III describes a lower cognitive functioning level where the patient shows withdrawal or vocalization to painful stimuli and turns toward or away from auditory stimuli.
- **C.** Level VI is indicative of patients who are inconsistently oriented and need moderate assistance, showing less cognitive recovery than the patient in the scenario.

**Q14.** A child with L3-L4 myelomeningocele demonstrates quadriceps and anterior tibialis activity but struggles with long-distance ambulation. Which of the following supports would best enhance functional mobility?
A. KAFOs and crutches with wheelchair for long distances
B. AFOs with crutches for community ambulation
C. RGOs with crutches for community ambulation
D. No external support with household ambulation

**Correct Answer:**
- **A.** At the L3-L4 level, the presence of quadriceps and tibialis anterior allows for short community ambulation with KAFOs and crutches. However, wheelchair use is necessary for longer distances due to potential fatigue.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **B.** AFOs do not provide adequate support for knee instability present at this level.
- **C.** RGOs are unnecessary, as quadriceps function can support standing and walking with less restrictive orthotics.
- **D.** No external support is unrealistic given the need for bracing to stabilize the knees.

**Q16.** Which pediatric orthopedic condition is most commonly associated with a Trendelenburg gait?
A. Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease
B. Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE)
C. Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH)
D. Osgood-Schlatter disease

**Correct Answer:**
- **C.** DDH affects hip stability, leading to weak hip abductors and a Trendelenburg gait due to an inability to maintain pelvic stability during single-leg stance.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease causes avascular necrosis of the femoral head and results in a painful limp, not necessarily a Trendelenburg gait.
- **B.** SCFE leads to an externally rotated gait, rather than a Trendelenburg sign.
- **D.** Osgood-Schlatter disease is a tibial apophysitis causing anterior knee pain, not affecting hip mechanics or gait.

**Q17.** In a patient with spastic diplegic cerebral palsy who is using an articulating ankle-foot orthosis (AFO), which muscle function is most essential for successful ambulation?
A. Hip abductor strength
B. Quadriceps control
C. Dorsiflexor strength and control
D. Plantarflexor endurance

**Correct Answer:**
- **C.** Dorsiflexor function is crucial in preventing foot drop, ensuring toe clearance during swing phase, and promoting a more efficient gait pattern.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** While hip abductors are important for lateral stability, they are not the primary determinant for AFO-assisted ambulation.
- **B.** Quadriceps are important, but the knee joint is not directly controlled by the AFO, making dorsiflexion control more critical.
- **D.** Plantarflexors contribute to push-off, but in spastic diplegia, dorsiflexor control is more relevant due to the tendency for excessive plantarflexion.

**Q19.** Which skeletal changes are commonly observed in Klumpke's palsy (C8-T1)?
A. Flattening of the glenoid fossa and posterior shoulder dislocation.
B. Hypertrophy of the olecranon and coronoid process with posterior dislocation of the radial head.
C. Posterior glenohumeral dislocation.
D. Elongating deformity of the coracoid process.

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** Klumpke's palsy is associated with hypertrophy of the olecranon and coronoid process, as well as elbow flexion contractures and potential posterior dislocation of the radial head.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Flattening of the glenoid fossa and posterior shoulder dislocation are associated with Erb's palsy, not Klumpke's palsy.
- **C.** Posterior glenohumeral dislocation is associated with total brachial plexus injury, not isolated Klumpke's palsy.
- **D.** Elongating deformity of the coracoid process is characteristic of Erb's palsy.

**Q20.** Which test is considered most useful in ruling out cervical myelopathy?
A. Babinski sign
B. Spurling's test
C. Hoffmann's sign
D. Finger escape sign

**Correct Answer:**
- **D.** The finger escape sign is a reliable indicator of cervical myelopathy, as it signifies dysfunction in intrinsic hand muscles due to cord compression.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** While a positive Babinski sign suggests an upper motor neuron lesion, it is not specific to cervical myelopathy.
- **B.** Spurling's test is used to diagnose cervical radiculopathy, not myelopathy.
- **C.** A positive Hoffmann's sign indicates an upper motor neuron lesion but lacks specificity in differentiating cervical myelopathy from other neurological conditions.
