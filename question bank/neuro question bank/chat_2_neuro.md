# NPTE Neuro Review – Neuromuscular Mixed Topics

**Q1.** A patient presents with double vision and tilts the head consistently to one side. When one eye is covered, the head tilt resolves. Which cranial nerve is MOST likely impaired?

A. CN III Oculomotor
B. CN IV Trochlear
C. CN V Trigeminal
D. CN VI Abducens

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** A trochlear nerve palsy causes weakness of the superior oblique muscle, resulting in vertical diplopia and compensatory head tilt to correct visual alignment. Covering one eye eliminates double vision, thus resolving the head tilt, confirming CN IV impairment.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Oculomotor palsy would lead to ptosis, pupil dilation, and impaired adduction/upward gaze, not primarily vertical diplopia corrected by head tilt.
- **C.** Trigeminal nerve affects facial sensation and muscles of mastication, not eye movement or double vision.
- **D.** Causes horizontal diplopia due to lateral rectus impairment, corrected by head turning rather than head tilting.

**Q2.** What type of spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is characterized by onset after 18 months, proximal muscle weakness, and the ability to ambulate independently until later stages?

A. SMA Type I
B. SMA Type II
C. SMA Type III
D. SMA Type IV

**Correct Answer:**
- **C.** SMA Type III, also known as Kugelberg-Welander disease, has an onset after 18 months, with proximal weakness, a Trendelenburg gait, and the ability to ambulate independently in earlier stages.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** SMA Type I (Werdnig-Hoffmann) has an earlier onset (0–3 months) and is rapidly progressive with severe hypotonia.
- **B.** SMA Type II presents before 18 months and typically involves more severe motor impairments and inability to ambulate.
- **D.** SMA Type IV is a rare, mild form of SMA that typically begins in early adulthood.

**Q3.** Which of the following findings is most concerning in a 10-month-old infant?

A. Persistent ATNR
B. Lack of independent sitting
C. Persistence of plantar grasp reflex
D. Absence of pincer grasp

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** By 9 months, an infant should be able to sit independently. Failure to achieve this milestone suggests neuromuscular delay, commonly seen in cerebral palsy or hypotonia syndromes.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** While abnormal if it persists beyond 6 months, ATNR alone does not strongly indicate pathology.
- **C.** This can persist until 12 months, making it a less concerning finding.
- **D.** The pincer grasp develops around 10–12 months, so absence at 10 months is still within normal range.

**Q5.** A physical therapist is evaluating a patient who recently sustained a cerebrovascular accident (CVA). The patient presents with right-sided hemiparesis. During the cognitive and behavioral screening, the therapist notes that the patient is very aware of their physical impairments, appears anxious about falling, and demonstrates a slow, cautious behavioral style when attempting new movement tasks. Based on this clinical presentation, which of the following additional impairments is this patient MOST likely to exhibit?

A. Left-sided unilateral neglect
B. Fluent (Wernicke's) aphasia
C. Quick, impulsive behavior with poor judgment
D. Difficulty grasping the whole idea of a task

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** Speech and language impairments, such as fluent (Wernicke's) aphasia, nonfluent (Broca's) aphasia, or global aphasia, are characteristic deficits of a dominant (typically left) hemisphere lesion.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Left-sided unilateral neglect is a visual-perceptual impairment associated with a right hemisphere lesion, which would typically present with left-sided hemiplegia.
- **C.** A quick, impulsive behavioral style and poor judgment are behavioral deficits associated with a right hemisphere lesion, contrasting with the slow, cautious style described in the prompt.
- **D.** Difficulty synthesizing information and grasping the whole idea of a task are intellectual deficits associated with a right hemisphere lesion.

**Q6.** A 42-year-old male with a complete spinal cord injury at T7 presents with intermittent urinary leakage and signs of renal deterioration. Urodynamics reveal detrusor contraction concurrent with high sphincter EMG activity. Which of the following best explains the underlying mechanism of his voiding dysfunction?

A. Suprapontine lesion leading to isolated detrusor hyperreflexia
B. Lesion at the conus medullaris resulting in detrusor areflexia
C. Detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia due to spinal cord lesion below the pons
D. Hypocontractile bladder due to diabetic autonomic neuropathy

**Correct Answer:**
- **C.** This is a classic case of detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia (DSD) — a failure of coordinated voiding in which both the detrusor contracts while the sphincter paradoxically tightens, causing intermittent urine flow, high bladder pressures, and potential renal damage. It is commonly seen in spinal lesions between the pons and sacral spinal cord, such as in T7 injuries.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** While suprapontine lesions can cause detrusor overactivity, they do not result in DSD, because coordination with the sphincter is preserved.
- **B.** Lesions at the conus cause areflexia and overflow incontinence, not involuntary detrusor contractions with sphincter dyssynergia.
- **D.** This would present as poor emptying with no reflex activity, not high-pressure dyssynergia and leakage.

**Q7.** Which of the following conditions must be met for a diagnosis of Definite ALS according to the El Escorial criteria?

A. Presence of upper motor neuron (UMN) and lower motor neuron (LMN) signs in one region with evidence of LMN involvement in another region confirmed by electromyography (EMG).
B. Presence of UMN and LMN signs in the bulbar region and at least two other spinal regions.
C. Presence of UMN signs in at least two regions, with UMN signs rostral to LMN signs.
D. Presence of UMN and LMN signs in one region or UMN signs in two or three regions such as monomelic ALS, progressive bulbar palsy, and primary lateral sclerosis.

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** This directly aligns with the El Escorial criteria for Definite ALS. The diagnosis requires the presence of UMN and LMN signs in the bulbar region and at least two other spinal regions, confirming widespread neurodegeneration affecting multiple regions.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** This describes Probable ALS, laboratory-supported, where EMG evidence supports LMN involvement in another region without fulfilling the stricter criteria for Definite ALS.
- **C.** This describes Probable ALS, which focuses on the presence of UMN signs in at least two regions with a particular anatomical distribution.
- **D.** This corresponds to Possible ALS, where the diagnostic certainty is less due to limited evidence of widespread neurodegeneration.

**Q8.** A 45-year-old female patient presents with progressive weakness in her right arm. The weakness is more noticeable when she tries to grip objects tightly. There is no sensory loss, but the deep tendon reflexes are decreased in the affected limb. Which of the following is the most likely lesion location?

A. Anterior horn of the spinal cord
B. Primary motor cortex (precentral gyrus)
C. Basal ganglia (putamen and globus pallidus)
D. Posterior limb of the internal capsule

**Correct Answer:**
- **A.** The combination of progressive weakness and reduced deep tendon reflexes suggests a lower motor neuron (LMN) lesion, which affects the anterior horn cells, as seen in conditions like amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) or poliomyelitis.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **B.** A cortical lesion would cause weakness but typically with hyperreflexia, not hyporeflexia.
- **C.** Damage here would affect movement regulation but not cause isolated weakness.
- **D.** This would lead to UMN signs, including spasticity and hyperreflexia.

**Q10.** A stroke patient demonstrates the following signs: ipsilateral third cranial nerve palsy, contralateral hemiplegia, and involuntary choreiform movements on the contralateral side. The lesion most likely localizes to which structure?

A. Midbrain tegmentum, affecting the red nucleus and corticospinal tract
B. Base of the midbrain, affecting the oculomotor nerve and corticospinal tract
C. Paramedian pons, affecting the abducens nucleus and corticospinal tract
D. Lateral medulla, affecting the spinal trigeminal nucleus and spinothalamic tract

**Correct Answer:**
- **A.** This combination of ipsilateral third nerve palsy, contralateral hemiplegia, and choreiform movements defines Benedikt syndrome, which localizes to the midbrain tegmentum involving both the red nucleus (causing ataxia and chorea) and corticospinal tract (causing hemiplegia).

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **B.** This describes Weber syndrome, which includes third nerve palsy and contralateral hemiplegia, but does not involve involuntary movements, as the red nucleus is spared.
- **C.** This localizes to Millard-Gubler syndrome, which causes sixth and seventh cranial nerve palsy with contralateral hemiplegia, but no oculomotor involvement or chorea.
- **D.** This describes Wallenberg syndrome, which presents with ipsilateral facial pain loss, contralateral body pain loss, ataxia, and dysphagia, but not oculomotor palsy or hemiplegia.

**Q11.** A 45-year-old individual presents with complaints of numbness and tingling in the right upper limb, accompanied by occasional weakness. The symptoms are exacerbated during activities involving overhead movements and sustained postures. Upon examination, the healthcare practitioner suspects thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS). Considering the anatomical sites for compression or entrapment in the thoracic outlet, which area would be most likely responsible for the observed symptoms?

A. Costoclavicular space between the clavicle superiorly and the first rib inferiorly
B. Axillary interval between the anterior deltopectoral fascia, the pectoralis minor, and the coracoid process
C. Interscalene triangle, bordered by the scalenus anterior and medius muscles and first rib
D. Structural anomalies, such as a cervical rib or malunion of a clavicular

**Correct Answer:**
- **C.** The interscalene triangle is a key anatomical site in the thoracic outlet region where the brachial plexus is vulnerable to compression or entrapment. The symptoms exacerbated by overhead movements align with the likelihood of compression in this area.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** The costoclavicular space is associated with compression or entrapment of the subclavian vessels rather than the brachial plexus. The symptoms described, such as numbness, tingling, and weakness during overhead movements, are more indicative of brachial plexus involvement.
- **B.** The axillary interval is not typically associated with thoracic outlet syndrome involving the brachial plexus. This space is more relevant to the axillary artery and vein. The symptoms described suggest a more proximal compression site.
- **D.** While structural anomalies can contribute to thoracic outlet syndrome, the symptoms described, particularly those aggravated by overhead movements, point more specifically to compression within the interscalene triangle.

**Q12.** A 45-year-old male with a severe traumatic brain injury exhibits abnormal posturing when stimulated. His upper extremities are flexed, and his lower extremities are extended. This pattern is characteristic of decorticate rigidity. At which level of the central nervous system is the lesion most likely located, and what is the typical limb posture observed in this condition?

A. Cerebrum; Lower extremities flexed, upper extremities flexed
B. Cerebrum; Lower extremities extended, upper extremities extended
C. Diencephalon; Lower extremities flexed, upper extremities extended
D. Diencephalon; Lower extremities extended, upper extremities flexed

**Correct Answer:**
- **D.** Decorticate rigidity occurs due to a lesion above the red nucleus (typically involving the internal capsule or diencephalon), which disrupts the corticospinal tract but spares the rubrospinal tract. The rubrospinal tract facilitates flexor tone in the upper extremities. The vestibulospinal and pontine reticulospinal tracts remain active, producing extensor tone in the lower extremities. This results in the classic flexed arms, extended legs posture seen in decorticate posturing.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** This combination is not characteristic of any specific postural reflex pattern. Decorticate rigidity involves flexion in upper limbs but extension in lower limbs, not flexion in both.
- **B.** This describes neither decorticate nor decerebrate rigidity. In both known abnormal postures, there is at least one limb group that flexes.
- **C.** This describes the reverse pattern of decorticate rigidity. Decerebrate rigidity, not decorticate, occurs with a lesion below the red nucleus (in the midbrain or pons), resulting in extension of both upper and lower extremities.

**Q16.** Which of the following statements regarding superficial reflex testing is correct?

A. Superficial reflexes are typically elicited by stimulating the motor cortex.
B. Superficial reflexes are diminished in upper motor neuron lesions.
C. Superficial reflexes are primarily mediated by the autonomic nervous system.
D. Superficial reflexes are absent in peripheral neuropathies.

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** Superficial reflexes, such as the abdominal and cremasteric reflexes, are usually elicited by stroking the skin or mucous membranes. Their primary utility is that they are abolished by pyramidal tract lesions, which typically result in the combination of increased deep tendon reflexes and decreased or absent superficial reflexes. In particular, the absence of superficial abdominal reflexes may support a lesion involving the thoracic spinal cord, especially in young and thin individuals.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Superficial reflexes are elicited by skin or mucous membrane stimulation, not by stimulating the motor cortex.
- **C.** Superficial reflexes are mediated by the somatic nervous system, not primarily by the autonomic nervous system.
- **D.** Superficial reflexes may be diminished in peripheral neuropathies, but they are not necessarily absent. Peripheral neuropathies affect peripheral nerves, while superficial reflexes are primarily controlled by spinal cord pathways and lower motor neurons.

**Q17.** A 3-year-old male with Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD) is brought to an outpatient physical therapy clinic by his parents. They report that he struggles to keep up with peers during play and has difficulty climbing stairs. The parents are concerned about his frequent falls, overall muscle weakness, and developmental delays. He has been prescribed corticosteroids and has regular cardiac check-ups. On examination, Gowers' sign is positive. Considering the patient's age and diagnosis, what is the MOST appropriate physical therapy intervention?

A. Work on improving developmental milestones to help the child catch up with the other kids.
B. Maintain the child's strength and muscle tone.
C. Prevent contractures and prescribe mobility aides.
D. Improve strength and balance control to reduce fall risk.

**Correct Answer:**
- **C.** In DMD, there is a progressive replacement of muscle fibers with fat and scar tissue, leading to muscle weakness and contractures. Physical therapy focused on preventing contractures is crucial because contractures limit joint mobility and can exacerbate functional impairments. Prescribing mobility aids is also critical as it helps maintain independence and mobility as the disease progresses.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** This is not the primary focus of intervention in DMD. The key issue is not a developmental delay per se, but rather a progressive muscle weakness. Interventions should prioritize maintaining physical function and addressing the specific muscular and joint challenges posed by DMD.
- **B.** The progressive nature of DMD inevitably leads to muscle weakness and loss of muscle tone, which cannot be fully halted or reversed. While maintaining strength and muscle tone is a part of management, it cannot be the primary focus given the disease's relentless progression.
- **D.** While it is important to address these issues, they are secondary to the more pressing concerns of contracture prevention and mobility aid prescription in the context of DMD.

**Q19.** Which of the following characteristics distinguishes CRPS type II from CRPS type I?

A. Pain disproportionate to the severity of injury
B. Develops after nerve injury
C. Symptoms are more marked distally in an extremity
D. Associated with abnormal sudomotor activity

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** CRPS Type II (causalgia) is specifically characterized by the presence of a clearly identifiable peripheral nerve injury. This direct association with nerve damage distinguishes it from CRPS Type I, which does not involve a definite nerve lesion.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Severe or disproportionate pain relative to the initial injury is characteristic of both CRPS type I and II. Therefore, this does not distinguish CRPS Type II from Type I.
- **C.** Symptoms beginning distally and progressing proximally are common in both CRPS Type I and II and do not distinguish between the two.
- **D.** Abnormal sweating or sudomotor activity is a typical feature of both types of CRPS, thus not useful in differentiating them.

**Q21.** A 45-year-old male with a severe traumatic brain injury exhibits abnormal posturing when stimulated. His upper extremities are flexed, and his lower extremities are extended. A 45-year-old male presents with difficulty moving the right eye downward when looking laterally and difficulty moving the left eye downward when looking medially. Which extraocular muscles are most likely impaired?

A. Right eye: Inferior Rectus; Left eye: Superior Oblique
B. Right eye: Superior Oblique; Left eye: Inferior Rectus
C. Right eye: Lateral Rectus; Left eye: Medial Rectus
D. Right eye: Superior Rectus; Left eye: Inferior Oblique

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** The Superior Oblique muscle (innervated by CN IV, Trochlear nerve) is responsible for depressing the eye when it is adducted and for intorsion. Difficulty moving the right eye downward in lateral gaze indicates a lesion affecting the right Superior Oblique. The Inferior Rectus muscle (innervated by CN III, Oculomotor nerve) depresses the eye when it is abducted. Difficulty moving the left eye downward in medial gaze points to left Inferior Rectus involvement.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** The Inferior Rectus primarily depresses the eye when it is abducted, not when looking laterally with the head in neutral. The Superior Oblique acts in depression and adduction, not in medial depression. This combination does not match the described movement deficits.
- **C.** The Lateral Rectus (CN VI) abducts the eye horizontally, and the Medial Rectus (CN III) adducts it. Neither muscle contributes significantly to vertical eye movement, making this choice incompatible with the presentation of impaired depression.
- **D.** The Superior Rectus elevates the eye, particularly in abduction. The Inferior Oblique elevates the eye in adduction. Both are primarily elevators, not depressors, making this the opposite of the described movement deficit.

**Q22.** A 45-year-old male with a severe traumatic brain injury exhibits abnormal posturing. A 3-year-old male with Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD). A 38-year-old patient, who regularly wears corrective lenses, is undergoing a cranial nerve examination. During the examination, the patient exhibits the absence of pupillary constriction in response to a light stimulus. Which possible abnormality does this finding suggest? What cranial nerve does this condition paralyze?

A. Lateral strabismus, CN II paralysis
B. Anisocoria, CN III paralysis
C. Horner's syndrome, CN III paralysis
D. Horner's syndrome, CN II paralysis

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** Anisocoria refers to unequal pupil sizes, often resulting from dysfunction of the parasympathetic fibers carried by the oculomotor nerve (Cranial Nerve III). The parasympathetic fibers control the constrictor muscles of the pupil. If CN III is paralyzed, the affected pupil will remain dilated and unresponsive to light. In this case, the sensory pathway (CN II) is intact, detecting the light, but the motor output (CN III) is impaired, preventing constriction.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Lateral strabismus, characterized by the outward deviation of the eye, is caused by CN III (Oculomotor) paralysis, not CN II (Optic). CN II damage results in visual field deficits or blindness, not extraocular muscle weakness or misalignment.
- **C.** Horner's syndrome is not caused by CN III paralysis. Instead, it results from disruption of the sympathetic pathway and presents with ptosis, miosis, and anhidrosis. CN III paralysis affects parasympathetic fibers and eye movements, not the sympathetic system.
- **D.** Horner's syndrome is due to sympathetic nervous system disruption, not optic nerve (CN II) dysfunction. CN II lesions affect vision (sensory input), leading to visual impairment or blindness, but do not cause autonomic signs such as ptosis, miosis, or anhidrosis.
