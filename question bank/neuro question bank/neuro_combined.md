**Q1.** A patient with a history of cerebrovascular accident (CVA) affecting the left hemisphere demonstrates difficulty with motor planning and execution of learned movements. This condition is BEST classified as:
A. Dysarthria
B. Ideomotor apraxia
C. Agnosia
D. Dysmetria

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** Ideomotor apraxia occurs when a patient is unable to perform a movement upon command despite understanding the task. This is typically associated with left hemisphere (dominant hemisphere) damage, which affects motor planning.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Dysarthria refers to difficulty in articulation due to neuromuscular impairment affecting speech production, not motor planning.
- **C.** Agnosia is the inability to recognize objects, sounds, or smells despite intact sensory function, which does not directly impact motor execution.
- **D.** Dysmetria is a cerebellar dysfunction leading to impaired coordination, resulting in overshooting or undershooting movements, rather than a deficit in motor planning.

**Q2.** A child with spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) Type I is most at risk for which of the following complications?
A. Progressive contractures and spasticity
B. Respiratory failure due to diaphragmatic paralysis
C. Distal sensory neuropathy
D. Progressive hypertrophy of lower limb muscles

**Correct Answer:**
- **B.** SMA Type I (Werdnig-Hoffmann disease) primarily affects the anterior horn cells, leading to progressive muscle weakness, particularly affecting respiratory muscles. Weakness of the diaphragm leads to hypoventilation and respiratory failure, a common cause of mortality.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** SMA causes flaccid hypotonia, not spasticity.
- **C.** SMA is a motor neuron disease, not a sensory disorder.
- **D.** Progressive hypertrophy of lower limb muscles describes pseudohypertrophy in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), not SMA.

**Q3.** A 42-year-old patient diagnosed with multiple sclerosis (MS) is referred for an exercise program. The patient experiences intermittent fatigue and occasional episodes of muscle stiffness. Which of the following exercise prescriptions is least appropriate for this patient?

B. A recumbent cycling program focusing on moderate-intensity cardiovascular training
C. An intermittent aerobic exercise plan incorporating frequent rest breaks

**Correct Answer:**

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **B.** Recumbent cycling is a suitable, low-impact option that allows for aerobic conditioning while minimizing fall risk.
- **C.** Intermittent rest is crucial in preventing excessive fatigue, a common issue in MS.
- **D.** A slow, progressive increase in workload is beneficial in managing symptoms and improving endurance without overexertion.

**Q5.** A 60-year-old patient is recovering in a rehabilitation facility after undergoing a total hip arthroplasty (THA) on their left hip. During the post-operative assessment, the physical therapist notes that the patient is experiencing difficulty with adduction and flexion of the left thigh. Which nerve injury is most likely associated with this condition, and what specific surgical procedure could have caused it?
A. Femoral nerve — hip arthroscopy
B. Sciatic nerve — posterior approach THA
C. Obturator nerve — total hip arthroplasty
D. Common peroneal nerve — THA

**Correct Answer:**
- **C.** The obturator nerve innervates the adductor muscles of the medial thigh and contributes to hip flexion via the gracilis and adductor longus/brevis. Injury to the obturator nerve typically results in weakness in thigh adduction and some difficulty with hip flexion. During THA, especially with medial or anteromedial surgical approaches, there is a risk of traction, compression, or direct trauma to the obturator nerve.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** The femoral nerve innervates the iliopsoas and the quadriceps. Injury causes weak hip flexion and significant knee extension weakness, not hip adduction deficits.
- **B.** The sciatic nerve innervates the hamstrings and muscles of the lower leg. Injury causes posterior thigh weakness, knee flexion deficits, and foot drop — not hip adduction weakness.
- **D.** The common peroneal nerve controls foot dorsiflexion and eversion. There is no involvement of hip adduction or thigh flexion in a peroneal nerve injury.

**Q7.** A 40-year-old individual has recently sustained a level D spinal cord injury (SCI) at the thoracic level (T12). The patient currently presents with partial motor and sensory deficits; they have partial loss of vibratory and position sense on the same side as the lesion, as well as a partial loss of pain and temperature sense on the opposite side of the lesion. Muscle testing revealed the following grades for each key muscle of the LE: Hip flexion (3/5), Hip abduction (2/5), Hip extension (2/5), Knee flexion (3/5), Knee extension (3/5), Ankle dorsiflexion (4/5), Ankle plantarflexion (3/5). C. Deep venous thrombosis (DVT); Implement active ankle pumps and calf muscle exercises
D. Venous Stasis; Compression therapy

**Correct Answer:**
- **C.** Individuals with an incomplete Level D spinal cord injury maintain motor function below the neurological level, and most key muscles below the neurological level have a muscle grade of 3 or more. Thus, they would still be able to perform ankle pumps with adequate training and feedback. Additionally, patients with spinal cord injuries are at an increased risk of developing deep venous thrombosis (DVT) due to impaired mobility and venous return.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Compression therapy is commonly used for musculoskeletal injuries but is not the primary focus in preventing DVT in individuals with SCI. Active ankle pumps and calf muscle exercises are more specific and effective.
- **B.** While active ankle pumps and calf muscle exercises are appropriate for addressing venous stasis and promoting blood circulation, venous stasis is less likely to occur as it is a chronic condition known as post-thrombotic syndrome and typically occurs as a sequel of DVTs.
- **D.** Venous stasis is less likely to occur as it is a chronic condition, known as post-thrombotic syndrome, and typically occurs as a sequel of DVTs. Compression therapy is not the primary focus in preventing DVT.

**Q8.** During a physical therapy examination, a 45-year-old patient displays an abnormal response to the patellar tendon tap. Based on the Deep Tendon Reflex (DTR) scoring system, which grading would best describe this response and confirm a potential Peripheral Nervous System (PNS) lesion?

**Correct Answer:**

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **B.** A 2+ response is considered normal in the DTR scoring system and would not indicate a diminished response.
- **C.** A 3+ response suggests an exaggerated response. Hyperreflexia might be true in case of CNS lesions.
- **D.** A 4+ response indicates a hyperactive response, which is not consistent with a diminished reflex related to a PNS lesion.

**Q10.** A 55-year-old male patient diagnosed with ALS is currently receiving treatment with Riluzole. During a recent physical therapy session, he reports increasing difficulty swallowing (dysphagia) accompanied by occasional choking episodes and cough during meals. On assessment, his tongue mobility is reduced and he reports fatigue during mealtimes. Working as part of the multidisciplinary rehabilitation team, what intervention should the team prioritize to address his swallowing difficulties given his diagnosis and disease progression?
A. Implement oropharyngeal exercises to improve tongue strength and coordination
B. Recommend a soft diet to minimize risk of choking during meals
C. Administer neuromuscular electrical stimulation to the throat muscles for enhanced coordination
D. Implement chin-tuck posture and other airway-protective compensatory strategies during meals to minimize risk of aspiration

**Correct Answer:**
- **D.** In ALS, bulbar involvement leads to progressive weakness of oropharyngeal musculature; the goal of dysphagia management is primarily safety, maintaining nutrition and hydration, and prolonging oral intake. The chin-tuck posture is a documented compensatory maneuver that narrows the airway entrance, reduces aspiration risk, and improves bolus control by repositioning anatomical structures.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** While tongue exercises can support swallowing in some populations, in ALS the progressive motor neuron loss limits benefit and may lead to fatigue; compensatory strategies take precedence.
- **B.** Diet modifications are part of management but are non-specific. The more specific intervention is posture/airway protection.
- **C.** NMES may be used experimentally, but evidence in ALS is very limited and managing fatigue and safety is more critical. It is not first-line in bulbar ALS dysphagia.

**Q11.** A patient presents with a left upper motor neuron (UMN) lesion affecting the face. Which of the following clinical findings is MOST likely to be observed?
A. Complete paralysis of the left side of the face
B. Weakness of the left lower and upper face
C. Weakness of the right upper face with normal lower facial movement
D. Weakness of the right lower face with sparing of the upper face

**Correct Answer:**
- **D.** In UMN lesions, such as a left-sided cortical stroke, the contralateral lower facial muscles are affected due to decussation of corticobulbar fibers. The upper face (forehead) receives bilateral cortical input, so it is typically spared in UMN lesions. Therefore, a left UMN lesion will cause right-sided lower facial weakness with preserved forehead movement.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Complete paralysis of the left side of the face is seen in a left lower motor neuron (LMN) lesion such as Bell's palsy, not a UMN lesion.
- **B.** Weakness of the left lower and upper face reflects a left LMN lesion, not a UMN lesion. The UMN lesion causes contralateral (right-sided) weakness, and only the lower face is affected.
- **C.** Isolated upper face weakness is unusual and would not result from a facial nerve lesion pattern.

**Q13.** A patient presents with mydriasis and an inability to move the eye medially. Which cranial nerve is most likely affected?
A. Trochlear nerve (CN IV)

C. Oculomotor nerve (CN III)

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** Damage to CN IV would cause vertical diplopia, particularly when looking downward, but not ptosis or pupillary dilation.
- **B.** Damage to CN VI would result in an inability to abduct the eye, but it would not affect the pupil or cause ptosis.
- **D.** The optic nerve transmits visual signals but does not control eye movement, the pupil, or eyelid function.

**Q18.** A premature infant born at 32 weeks demonstrates hypotonia and poor reflex irritability in the NICU. Based on neonatal developmental care principles, which intervention is MOST appropriate to support neuromotor development?
A. Tactile stimulation to promote neuromuscular activation
B. Gentle PROM to prevent joint stiffness
C. Positioning in flexion-based postures to mimic the intrauterine environment
D. Begin weight-bearing exercises to develop strength

**Correct Answer:**
- **C.** Positioning with flexion, containment, and midline orientation is the primary early intervention for premature infants with hypotonia. At 32 weeks gestation, physiologic flexion and postural control are not fully developed. Flexion-based positioning promotes neuromotor organization, supports physiologic stability, reduces energy expenditure, and facilitates normal movement patterns.

**Incorrect Answers:**
- **A.** While gentle sensory input may be used selectively, active stimulation to increase muscle activation is not the primary intervention for premature infants with hypotonia. Preterm infants have immature sensory systems and low neurologic thresholds; excessive stimulation may cause physiologic instability.
- **B.** Passive range of motion may be appropriate for maintaining joint integrity, but it does not address the underlying issue of neuromotor immaturity.
- **D.** Weight-bearing interventions are not appropriate for a medically fragile premature infant. Interventions must prioritize physiologic stability, neuroprotection, and developmental positioning.
